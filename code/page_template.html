The turnpike towns

From 1663 to the 1830s, more than a thousand turnpike trusts took over England's main roads, put up gates, charged tolls and mended the road with the money. Their milestones still stand on the verges. Did being on a turnpike make a town grow?

Dr Tristan Hotham, Independent researcher · October 2026 · Data: Wikipedia lists of turnpike trusts; Wikidata and OpenStreetMap milestones and toll houses; census populations 1801 to 1901 via Wikidata and A Vision of Britain through Time; OS Open Names

In {{trusts.first_year|raw}} Parliament let the justices of Hertfordshire, Cambridgeshire and Huntingdonshire put up toll gates on the Great North Road, at Wadesmill, Caxton and Stilton, and spend the money on the road. It was the first turnpike. Over the next century and a half, local worthies petitioned Parliament for road after road, each run by its own trust: a body of trustees who could borrow, set up gates, collect tolls and pay surveyors. At their peak in the 1830s, Wikipedia's article says, over 1,000 trusts looked after some 30,000 miles of road in England and Wales.

The turnpikes carried the coaching age. The first mail coach left Bristol for London in 1784; Telford's rebuilt Holyhead Road cut the London mail's journey from 45 hours to 27. Inns, ostlers and horse dealers lined the routes. Then the railways came, and the coaching towns emptied. So I asked a simple question with the data I could get: were the towns on the turnpikes the ones that grew?

The honest short answer: turnpikes reached almost everywhere quickly, and once you allow for the fact that bigger places got them first, I can find little sign that being named in a turnpike trust made a place grow faster in the decades before the railway. The milestones tell a story of their own, though, and it is not quite the one you might expect.

    02 · Turnpike maniaA boom in the 1750s and 1760s

    Wikipedia's eight regional lists name {{trusts.unique|int}} English turnpike trusts, {{trusts.with_year|int}} of them with the year of their first act. The lists are marked incomplete, and the Sussex trusts have no dates, but the shape is clear. After a slow start (just {{trusts.n_1700s|words}} new trusts in the ten years from 1700), trust-making took off in the 1750s: {{trusts.n_1750s|int}} trusts in that decade and {{trusts.n_1760s|int}} in the 1760s. Between 1750 and 1772 alone, {{trusts.n_1750_1772|int}} trusts were set up, {{trusts.share_1750_1772|pct0}} of all those with a date. By 1800, {{trusts.by1800|pct0}} of the trusts existed. Only {{trusts.after1830|int}} were founded after 1830, when the railways began.

    The trusts did not march out from London in a neat wave. The great roads out of London were turnpiked early, and towns named in trusts within 50 km of London had their first trust in a median year of {{wave.bands.0.median|raw}}. But beyond 200 km the median was {{wave.bands.3.median|raw}}, only about a decade later. Distance from London explains very little of the timing (Spearman rank correlation {{wave.rho|f2}} across {{wave.n_towns|int}} towns). The North West was the latecomer, with a median founding year of {{regions.7.median|raw}}: {{nw_1790_1829|pct0}} of its dated trusts were founded between 1790 and 1829, many of them for the roads between Lancashire's growing mill towns.

    Some towns were the hub of a whole web of trusts. {{named.hub_top.0.name|raw}} appears in the titles of {{named.hub_top.0.n|int}} trusts, {{named.hub_top.1.name|raw}} in {{named.hub_top.1.n|int}} and {{named.hub_top.2.name|raw}} in {{named.hub_top.2.n|int}}. In all, {{named.places|int}} places are named in at least one trust title, {{named.towns|int}} of them towns or cities on today's map. In England, {{validate.named|int}} of the {{validate.towns|int}} places that Ordnance Survey calls a town or city ({{validate.named_share|pct0}}) are named in a trust. The rest were mostly reached by roads named after their neighbours.

    03 · The coaching ageFaster roads, then the railway

    The point of a turnpike was a better road, and the coaching trade made the most of it. These dates come from the Wikipedia articles on turnpike trusts, mail coaches and toll houses.

    • 1663The first turnpike: toll gates on the Great North Road at Wadesmill, Caxton and Stilton, run by the county justices.
    • 1706The first true trust, for a stretch of the London to Chester road, under the Bedfordshire and Buckinghamshire Roads Act.
    • 1750s to 1770s"Turnpike mania": trusts for the cross-routes between the great roads, then for connecting roads, new bridges and the growing industrial districts.
    • 1773The Turnpike Roads Act makes statutory provision for milestones along turnpike roads.
    • 1784John Palmer's first mail coach runs from Bristol to London in 16 hours. By the end of 1785 mail coaches run to Norwich, Liverpool, Leeds, Dover, Portsmouth, Poole, Exeter, Gloucester, Worcester, Holyhead and Carlisle.
    • 1815 to 1826Telford rebuilds the Holyhead Road; the London mail's journey falls from 45 hours to 27.
    • 1830sThe peak: over 1,000 trusts, around 30,000 miles of road, almost 8,000 toll gates and side bars. In 1840 there were over 5,000 toll houses in England.
    • 1839 to 1843The Rebecca Riots in Wales: rioters, often men dressed as women, break the hated toll gates.
    • 1840sThe railway era "spelt disaster for most turnpike trusts". The Bolton and Blackburn trust took £3,998 in tolls in 1846; after the railway between the two towns opened, it took £1,185 in 1849.
    • 1870s to 1888Parliament closes the trusts one by one, and the toll houses are sold off. The Local Government Act 1888 hands main roads to the new county councils.

    The turnpikes were a private fix for a public problem: local people borrowed against future tolls to mend their own road.

    04 · What survives{{traces.total|int}} milestones and toll houses

    The trusts had to tell travellers how far they had come, and many of the stones and cast-iron posts they set up are still there. Wikidata and OpenStreetMap together record {{traces.milestones|int}} milestones, {{traces.mileposts|int}} mileposts, {{traces.guide|int}} guide stones and {{traces.tollhouses|int}} toll houses in the UK: {{traces.england|int}} in England, {{traces.wales|int}} in Wales, {{traces.scotland|int}} in Scotland and {{traces.ni|int}} in Northern Ireland. {{traces.listed|int}} are listed buildings or scheduled monuments.

    Half the survivors stand within {{traces.aroad_median|int}} m of an A-road. Most are dated only to a century, and most of those to the 19th: the coaching age was when trusts replaced old stones and added cast-iron posts. Of the {{traces.exact_year|int}} with an exact year, the median is {{traces.exact_median|raw}}.

    Where they survive is uneven. West Yorkshire has {{counties.top.0.d|f1}} milestones or mileposts per 100 km², Staffordshire {{counties.top.1.d|f1}} and Cheshire {{counties.top.2.d|f1}}. Bedfordshire, which had some of the very first trusts, has just {{counties.bottom.5.d|f1}}, and Northamptonshire {{counties.bottom.4.d|f1}}. England as a whole has {{counties.england_density|f1}} per 100 km², Wales {{counties.wales_density|f1}} and Scotland {{counties.scotland_density|f1}}. Some of this is real loss to road widening and decay; much of it is recording. About half of the Wikidata milestones carry a listed-building designation, and how thoroughly the rest have been entered varies from county to county. Scotland's milestones are barely recorded here at all.

    Still, the survivors do follow the old network. Across {{grid.cells|int}} squares of 20 km by 20 km in England, squares with more places named in trusts also have more surviving milestones (Spearman correlation {{grid.rho|f2}}). Milestones are a fair if patchy trace of where the turnpikes ran, but a count of survivors, not of the roads.

    05 · Did the turnpike make the town?Not that I can see

    To test growth I need places with population counts from census to census. The best set I have is {{panel.n|int}} places in England and Wales with census figures for 1801, 1831 and 1851 ({{panel.n_wikidata|int}} from Wikidata, {{panel.n_vob|int}} inland towns from A Vision of Britain through Time). It is not a random sample: {{panel.fens|int}} of them are in Cambridgeshire, Huntingdonshire and the fens, and {{panel.southwest|int}} in Dorset, Somerset and Devon, because that is where Wikidata's editors have entered parish series. Most are villages: the median place had {{panel.median_pop1801|int}} people in 1801.

    I measured exposure to the turnpikes in two ways: whether the place is named in a trust founded by 1800 ({{panel.named1800|int}} places), and whether a surviving milestone, milepost or toll house stands within 1 km ({{panel.near|int}} places). Growth is the average yearly change in population, in percentage points.

    Median yearly growth, with and without a turnpike
    Filled dot: places with the feature. Open dot: places without it. Per cent a year.

    At first sight the turnpike places did grow faster in the coaching heyday: from 1801 to 1831 the places named in a trust grew by a median {{compare.named1800.g1801_1831.yes|f2}}% a year, against {{compare.named1800.g1801_1831.no|f2}}% for the rest (Mann-Whitney {{compare.named1800.g1801_1831.p|p}}). But the named places were the bigger market towns, and bigger places grew faster everywhere. Once I allow for size in 1801 and for the region, the gap shrinks to {{models.g1801_1831.named1800.b|f2}} points a year, with a 95% interval from {{models.g1801_1831.named1800.lo|f2}} to {{models.g1801_1831.named1800.hi|f2}}. A milestone within 1 km makes no difference ({{models.g1801_1831.near.b|f2}} points, {{models.g1801_1831.near.lo|f2}} to {{models.g1801_1831.near.hi|f2}}).

    The turnpike effect on growth, allowing for size and region
    Difference in yearly growth (percentage points) from a regression with log population in 1801 and area. Line: 95% interval.

    If anything, the turnpike places lost ground when the railways arrived. From 1831 to 1851 the named places grew {{models.g1831_1851.named1800.b|abs2}} points a year more slowly than similar places (interval {{models.g1831_1851.named1800.lo|f2}} to {{models.g1831_1851.named1800.hi|f2}}, {{models.g1831_1851.named1800.p|p}}), and their growth fell back by a median {{change.named|abs2}} points between the two periods, against {{change.other|abs2}} for the rest ({{change.p|p}}). That fits the coaching story, but it is a weak result from a small, lopsided sample, and I would not lean on it.

    Stilton: a Great North Road village, 1801 to 1901
    Population as a multiple of 1801. Green: Stilton. Grey: median of the {{panel.n|int}} places. Dashed: median of the places named in a trust by 1800.

    Stilton, on the first turnpike of all, shows the pattern people imagine. It had {{stilton.1801|int}} people in 1801 and {{stilton.1831|int}} in 1831, then stalled at {{stilton.1851|int}} in 1851 and fell to {{stilton.1881|int}} by 1881 and {{stilton.1901|int}} by 1901, back where it started. But most rural places in this sample peaked around 1851 and shrank after, coaching or not, as people left the land for the towns: {{peaks.other_by1851|pct0}} of the places not named in a trust were at their largest in 1851 or earlier. Stilton's fall is steeper than most, which is suggestive, not proof.

    The big towns tell the same story. {{big.named|int}} of the {{big.n|int}} largest towns of 1801 (London aside) are named in a trust title, so there is almost no contrast to test. Among the {{big.n_g|int}} with figures for both 1801 and 1861, neither an early first trust (Spearman {{big.rho_first|f2}}) nor the number of trusts naming the town ({{big.rho_hub|f2}}) is linked with faster growth. Manchester, Liverpool and Leeds grew for reasons of their own, and their turnpikes followed the traffic.

    06 · LimitsWhat this can and cannot show

    No map of the roads. The best evidence would be a dated map of every turnpike road, and one exists: the turnpike road GIS for England and Wales, 1700 to 1838, by Rosevear, Bogart, Shaw-Taylor and Satchell (openICPSR 195126). I could not obtain it for this study. My code is written to use it if it is supplied. Without it, a place counts as "named" only if its name is in a trust's title, which misses every town that a turnpike ran through without naming.

    The lists are incomplete. Wikipedia flags its turnpike lists as incomplete; {{trusts.sussex_no_year|int}} Sussex trusts have no date, and Wales and Scotland have no dated lists at all. Seven of the eight lists were read with an automated web reader; for the South East list, held on disk as wikitext, the reader matched all {{trusts.fidelity.n|int}} Berkshire and Kent rows I checked, name, year and act.

    Milestones are survivors. They show where stones were set up, then kept, then recorded. Recording varies hugely between counties.

    A small, lopsided growth sample. {{panel.n|int}} places, mostly villages in two regions, and populations on census parish boundaries that changed. Associations, not causes: trusts were set up on roads where traffic already was.

    How I did this

    Trusts. I took the tables of Wikipedia's eight lists of turnpike trusts by English region: trust name, the year of its first act and that act's short title. Rows without a trust name (later acts of the same trust) were dropped and a trust listed under two counties was kept once, leaving {{trusts.unique|int}}. I split each trust's name into place words ("Ashbourne and Yoxall Bridge" gives Ashbourne and Yoxall Bridge) and matched each to an OS Open Names town, village or hamlet of exactly that name, preferring one in the trust's region, then a town, then the one nearest the trust's other places. {{trusts.placed|int}} trusts ({{trusts.placed_share|pct0}}) have at least one placed name.

    Traces. Wikidata items in the UK that are milestones or toll houses, and OpenStreetMap features tagged as historic milestones, mileposts, toll houses or toll gates, deduplicated within 40 m. Wikidata's dates of exactly 1701, 1801 or 1901 are century dates. Distances to A-roads use OpenStreetMap trunk and primary roads with an A number.

    Growth. Census figures for 1801, 1831, 1851 and 1881 from Wikidata (places with fewer than 50 people in 1801 left out) and A Vision of Britain through Time. Yearly growth is 100 × ln(P2/P1) divided by the years between. The regressions use ordinary least squares with robust standard errors, log population in 1801 and three areas (fens, south west, other).
